### What is claimed, and what is not

**No CPU-cost reduction is declared for any workload, and none will be claimed whatever the workloads read** (`WIN_WORKLOAD=none`). The change's gain is not in the cycles one request costs: it removes two takings of a lock that every thread of tokio's multi-threaded runtime shares, and what that gives is requests a second and latency when many cores serve handlers that wait. The rule for that is item 2.3 (time), and it was measured on this form: on a 48-CPU host with the server on 24 CPUs, base `e2d0c464e` against head `bf13f4822`, a route whose handler sleeps 10 ms answered 13.0% to 31.4% more requests a second where a difference resolved (24 or 48 workers, 8,192 or 32,000 connections), and nothing resolved at 4 or 16 workers, at 1,024 connections or with one single-threaded runtime a core. That record is one build of each side on one host, placement not varied (tag `evidence/deadline-timer-4`, `deadline-timer-4/time48/`). Those two commits hold the code of this plan's base and head but for five tests, three comment lines and a recorder under `cfg(test)` that the head gained (by `git diff` over what a binary is built from).

**What this plan reads is the cost, and who pays it.** A connection keeps one timer for its requests' deadlines once two of them have waited, and a deadline gives it back still set. While any kept timer is set and in tokio's timer wheel, the runtime's driver reads the clock and works out a timeout each time it parks, about 190 instructions a park (counted). That falls on every request that driver serves in that time, on any connection, not on the connection that waited: for a deadline's length (30 s by default) after the connection's last request that waited. `main` pays the same per park in an app with a standing timer of its own, and on every park made while a request waits. Where one park serves one request it is about 1% of a request's instructions and, in the time measurement, 4.0% and 4.3% of the server's CPU time a request (about 0.7 microseconds); where one park serves many requests it is spread over them. Those two figures are the head over the base with the idle connection there, and are the ones the maintainer's decisions quote. By the rule that measurement wrote down for a workload of two conditions (a build's change from no idle connection to one, against the base's own) the set timer's part reads less: +2.64% of the server's CPU time a request on the multi-threaded runtime (noise bar 3.57%, 10 rounds of 10 up, not resolved) and +3.29% on the single-threaded one (bar 3.05%, 10 of 10 up, resolved).

### Workloads

Seven, each one the change touches: every request `corten::serve` answers is polled through the wrapper the change puts around its connection. Each is sent to one probe program: the stage header checks' probe (tag `evidence/stage-header-checks-2002`, features `json` and `compress`) with two routes added for the first declaration, `GET /wait` and `GET /unstaged`, and, for this one, its runtime built by hand so that it can be started in either of two configurations: `plain`, one single-threaded tokio runtime, as every earlier run of this probe; and `plain-mt`, tokio's multi-threaded runtime with one worker, which is what `#[tokio::main]` builds on the one CPU a turn gives the server. The first five workloads are `plain`'s configuration.

The expectations below are instructions a request by callgrind on another app, head `bf13f4822` over base `e2d0c464e`, round 1 and round 2, medians of three counts. Instructions are not cycles; the margin is read in cycles.

1. `plain`, +1%: `GET /private` with no request header of its own, on kept-alive connections, `wrk -t2 -c64`. The record page for one visitor, about 6 KB. A staged request that does not wait: its deadline never starts a timer. Touched: the connection's poll goes through the wrapper (+7 and +9 instructions of about 19,980).
2. `wait`, +1%: `GET /wait`, the same page from a handler that sleeps 1 ms first (`tokio::time::sleep`), so every request's deadline waits; kept-alive, `wrk -t2 -c64`. Touched: a connection's first two deadlines make their own timers as before, at the second the connection makes its timer, and from its third request the deadline moves the connection's timer (-134 and -132 instructions of about 23,810).
3. `close`, +1%: `GET /wait` with `Connection: close`, so `wrk -t2 -c64` opens a connection for each request (`lua/close.lua`). A connection whose only request waits: the deadline makes its own timer as before and tells the connection it waited, and the connection makes none (+152 and +149 of about 35,050; +81 to +199 over every count of it).
4. `unstaged`, +1%: `GET /unstaged`, a route that says `unstaged`, so it has no deadline; a short `str` out; kept-alive, `wrk -t2 -c64`. Touched: the wrapper only, on the cheapest request there is (+17 and +18 of about 12,170, counted on the earlier base's code). It stands for every route with no deadline.
5. `after`, +1%: `GET /private` as `plain` sends it, on 64 kept-alive connections that have each first sent requests for `/wait`, so that each has its kept timer set. **What it reads, said exactly:** the cost of timers left set, spread over the requests one park of the driver serves when 64 connections are busy. With 64 busy connections the driver parked about 0.02 times a request in the counts, and the row read +25 and +29 instructions of about 19,200 on the single-threaded runtime (the third review's own count of it read -10 and +21). **It does not read the cost at one request a park;** `beside` and `besidemt` do. It is kept as a workload the change touches that was declared before; a pass on it says nothing of the worst case.

   *What a turn sends* (`lua/after.lua`). Each of `wrk`'s two threads answers its first 512 calls for a request with `GET /wait` and every call after with `GET /private`, which is the URL on `wrk`'s command line and byte for byte `plain`'s request. A thread has 32 connections, so that is about 16 requests that wait for each connection. `wrk` uses the first thread's first call to check the script and sends nothing for it, so a turn sends 1,023 requests for `/wait` and then, to its end, requests for `/private`. That start is inside every counted turn of both sides.

   *How the run verifies it.* `wrk`'s script cannot count a connection's requests, so: (a) before anything is counted, `state.sh` runs three whole turns of this load on each side's unpadded binary with `tcpdump` recording every packet with data sent to the server's port and no counter read, and `state.mjs` stops the run unless there are exactly 64 connections, each sending at least three requests for `/wait` before its first for `/private` and none after, no packet that begins anything else, exactly as many requests in the capture as `wrk`'s threads sent, every connection's TCP sequence numbers in step, no packet dropped and no capture cut inside a record; (b) in every turn the script's `done()` prints no result line unless both threads went past their 512th call, and a turn with no result line fails its placement; (c) `report.mjs` reads no results of `after` without `state.sh`'s verdicts for each side the part reads. These show watched turns, not counted ones: a counted turn in which a connection sent fewer than three is possible and would not be seen, and would read the workload low, never high.
6. `beside`, **+5%**: `GET /private` as `plain` sends it, on **one** kept-alive connection (`wrk -t1 -c1` on one CPU), beside **one more connection that asked for `/wait` three times and then stays open and idle**. The probe's configuration `plain`, the single-threaded runtime. It is the worst case of the maintainer's decision in its single-threaded shape: the busy connection never waits and makes no timer; the idle one's timer was set by its third wait and stands for 30 s; the driver parks about once a request (1.00 to 1.03 parks a request in the counts), and each park pays for the standing timer. Expected: +192 and +193 instructions of about 19,980, about 1.0%; the time measurement read +4.3% in the server's CPU time a request.

   *How a turn sets it up, and how the run verifies it* (`idle.mjs`, started by `run-pair-fresh.sh`). Every turn is on a freshly started server. Before the turn's counters start, a second process opens one connection, sends `GET /wait` three times, each after the answer before it has arrived whole, requires three answers of `200` with a `Content-Length` and no `Connection: close`, prints a line saying so, and then sends nothing. `perf stat` starts only after that line, so the three waits are no part of the count. After the counters stop the process must still be running with its connection open and nothing received since, and says so in a second line when it is stopped. A turn without both lines fails its placement, and `report.mjs` reads no placement of these two workloads unless its log holds each of its 18 turns once and, in each turn's own lines, the connection set up once and held once. A turn is 3 s of load on a process that lives under 6 s, and the probe's deadline is the default 30 s, so the timer does not come due inside a turn.

   *How the run shows the timer in the wheel.* The idle connection's two lines do not show it. The timeout the server gives its wait for an event (`epoll_wait`) does, since tokio's driver waits with no timeout while its wheel is empty and with one while any timer stands in it. Two checks, both declared now:

   (a) *Watched turns* (`park.sh`, `park.mjs`). Before anything is counted, for each side the part reads (the base in the pilot; the base and the head in the gated part), three whole turns are run as a turn is (the side's unpadded binary freshly started on CPU 2, the idle connection set up, the same load for 3 s) with `strace` recording every `epoll_wait` of the server and no counter read. A wait given a timeout of 0 cannot block and is passed over (the multi-threaded scheduler looks for events that way on both sides). Of the waits that can block there must be at least 100, **every one with no timeout on the base and every one with a timeout on the head**; anything else stops the run before its first counted turn, and `report.mjs` reads no results of these two workloads without the three verdicts of each side. This was shown to work beforehand in a container, on this plan's base and head (the history's last entry): the base's waits all had no timeout and the head's all had one of 25.3 to 28.5 s, 1.00 waits that can block a request on `plain` and 0.97 on `plain-mt`; each side's record read by the other's rule was refused. **Those figures are printed and not gated:** `park.mjs` passes the head on any timeout above 0 on at least 100 waits that can block, with no bound near the deadline's length and none on the waits a request.

   (b) *The counted turns: a floor on the head's instructions.* A watched turn is not a counted one, so the counted turns must show the state themselves, by a diagnostic every turn already counts. **For `beside` and for `besidemt`, at the median across the change comparison's K placements of each placement's median over its 8 pairs, the head must run at least 95 more user instructions a request than the base (`instructions:u`). Under that the workload is not read as declared, which does not pass, whatever its cycles read.** The floor can refuse a pass and nothing else: a miss stays a miss and not resolved stays not resolved. Why 95: a set timer costs about 190 instructions a park (callgrind) and a closed loop on one connection parks about once a request, so turns in the state show about 190 more and turns out of it about 8 (the wrapper alone); 95 is half the one and far from the other, in instructions because the cost is one park's and not a share of the request.

   What neither shows: counted turns out of the state that the medians hide, and **the floor lets through up to half**. By the fifth review's made-up results on this kit's `report.mjs`, 15 placements of 30 at 190 more instructions and 15 at 0 (median 95) read "meets +5%", 14 and 16 read not as declared, and half of every placement's pairs at 190 with half at 0 also meets. So up to half the placements, or half of each placement's pairs, can be out of the state with the floor met, and cycles would then read low by up to about half the cost, never high; each turn's two idle-connection lines make that unlikely and do not exclude it. That a third wait sets the timer and that it stands a deadline's length is also held by the crate's tests.
7. `besidemt`, **+5%**: `beside` on the probe's configuration `plain-mt`. It is the worst case in its multi-threaded shape as far as a turn's one server CPU allows: tokio's multi-threaded scheduler and its way of parking on the driver, with one worker. Expected: +193 and +187 instructions of about 20,620, about 0.9% (by callgrind on another app's multi-threaded runtime; the fourth review reads that count as made with more than one worker, and the record does not say). The watched turns and the floor of workload 6 hold for it as for `beside`. **What it is not:** the time measurement's multi-threaded shape had 24 workers on 24 CPUs, where it read +4.0%; a turn here pins the server to one CPU, so **this workload reads tokio's multi-threaded scheduler with one worker and does not read the 24-worker shape**, and no workload of this plan reads more than one worker.

### What both parts share: builds, placements, turns, hosts

Each side is compiled once on the measuring host from an emptied target directory, with the side's own `Cargo.lock`, the repository's release profile, rustc 1.100.0 and its LLD, zstd linked to the system's libzstd, as the stage header checks' `build.sh` does; a placement is the probe's recorded final link run again with a pad first in `.text`, one function of n bytes that nothing calls, so every other function moves by the pad and keeps its order. The build's checks stop the run before anything is measured: the link run again unpadded is byte for byte the build's own binary; each placement has exactly that binary's functions and the pad, the pad first, every function moved by exactly the pad's size and every pair of neighbours still next to each other in order; every binary's SHA-256 is distinct; and (`verify.sh`) each placement answers the workloads' requests as its side's unpadded binary does. `build.sh` and `run.sh` stop if a side's source is not the commit `commits.env` names for it.

**Code aligned beyond 16 bytes: the fifth point.** Item 2's fifth point asks of every placement run, "with code aligned beyond 16 bytes or without": "the unpadded link the first point compares with the build's own binary is made with LLD's map (`-Wl,-Map`). Before anything is measured, the run records from that map, and the pull request reports, each input section of `.text` aligned beyond 16 bytes, in order with its alignment, and every symbol the map lists in `.text` from the first of them on", and each side's map kept with the run's results. In both parts, for each side a part builds: `build.sh` runs the recorded link again unpadded with `-Wl,-Map` and requires it byte for byte the build's own binary; `maprec.mjs` writes the record from the map (`aligned-<side>.txt`); `run.sh` puts the record and the map (`map-<side>.map.gz`) in the results before any turn; `report.mjs` reads no results whose map is missing, empty or not an LLD map, or whose record is not, byte for byte, the record `maprec.mjs` makes again from the kept map; and the pull request reports the record with each part's results. The plan does not say in advance what the record holds.

The point goes on: "Where either side has such a section, the rest of this point holds for both": a second pad laid last in `.text`, each placement's map checked against LLD's rule, and three conditions under which a side has no reading across placements for any workload. **This kit has no second pad and makes none of those checks.** So if either side's record shows an input section of `.text` aligned beyond 16 bytes, the build stops, nothing is counted, and under this kit that side has no reading across placements, which does not pass; the way on is another kit under a new plan, and whether to take it is the maintainer's decision. A plan "may not build the C code with flags chosen for the measurement that leave no such section", and this one does not: the probe's build makes no C archive of its own (a build that does stops) and links the system's libzstd. A look that is not that record found none on either side (tag `evidence/deadline-timer-5`, `deadline-timer-5/look/`: the base and `850d39ac7`, built once off this plan's hosts, no counter read). Started as the kit starts a server, each side has three threads as `plain` and four as `plain-mt`, the fourth its one worker (`deadline-timer-5/trial/threads.txt`).

**Pads.** The pad lists for K placements are those `pads.mjs K` of the sizing kit prints (tag `evidence/placement-sizing-1999`), which meet the rule for an A/A's two lists at any K to 254. A workload's lists are those of its own K, which the kit writes and checks (`padlists.mjs`). Placement i of an A/A is **base with pad p_i against base with pad q_i**. Placement i of the change is **base with pad p_i against head with pad p_i**.

**Turns.** A placement is R = 8 recorded pairs after one that is not: every turn, the unrecorded pair's included, on a freshly started build pinned to CPU 2; the load for 3 s (`wrk -t2 -c64` on CPUs 4 and 5; for `beside` and `besidemt`, `wrk -t1 -c1` on CPU 4); the driver, `perf` and the idle connection's process on CPU 7; A's and B's order turned every pair; jemalloc 5.3.0 preloaded with `MALLOC_CONF=background_thread:true,tcache_max:524288`. ### Part 1: the pilot

For each of the seven workloads w, on its host: `pilot-w`, an A/A of the base, **K = 100 placements**, the size the Choice gives a pilot "unless the plan says why another of at least 30 that the pads allow serves"; R = 8; the pad lists `pads.mjs 100`; the order from seed 7401 for `plain`, 7402 for `wait`, 7403 for `close`, 7404 for `unstaged`, 7405 for `after`, 7406 for `beside` and 7407 for `besidemt` (7301 to 7304, 7311 to 7315 and 7321 to 7325 were the earlier writings').

The pilot is read for the spread of its 100 placement medians (each the median of a placement's 8 per-pair changes, B / A - 1 in user plus kernel cycles a request) and for nothing else. Whether it resolves +1% does not matter. It is **eligible** when the three conditions below hold, worked out by the sizing kit's `model.mjs`, unchanged, called as that kit's `report.mjs` calls it (20,000 permutations, seed 1999, for each of the two tests):

1. All 100 placements were read with all 8 pairs. None is run again, so a pilot in which one placement fails a check or its time limit is not eligible.
2. Neither the lag-1 autocorrelation of the medians in run order nor Spearman's correlation of the medians with run order is at p < 0.05, each by permutation.
3. Zero is inside the interval from the 42nd to the 59th of the 100 sorted medians.

From an eligible pilot, `size.mjs` gives the chance that an A/A of K placements fails one of its two readings at +1% under the normal model and under a smoothed resample of the pilot's medians. If Shapiro-Wilk or Anderson-Darling rejects normality at 5%, only the smoothed resample is kept; otherwise both are. The A/A of `beside` and `besidemt` is sized at +1% like the others: their wider margin is the change's, not the A/A's.

**The rule for K, fixed now and word for word the first declaration's.** Each workload has its own K: **the smallest K from 30 to 254 at which every kept model's count of failing draws is at most 10,000 of 200,000**, read with `sizePilot(readPilot(dir), { K: [K], draws: 200000, seed: 1983 })` as `Math.round(rate * draws)` for that K itself. The one-decimal rates `size.mjs` prints decide nothing. A K found this way is a model's estimate; the gated run's own A/A decides what the run resolves. The K's from 30 up are tried with the two functions that call runs for a K, with its arguments (`failRateNormal(K, s, 200000, 1983)` and `failRateSmoothed(pilot, K, 200000, 1983)` of `model.mjs`), and the K found is then read once with the call itself (`pilot.mjs --confirm`), whose counts are the ones published.

A pilot that is not eligible sizes nothing, and so does one for which no K to 254 meets the rule. That workload is then not read across placements under this plan, which does not pass. Choice 72 lets a plan "read another under a new plan, at a larger K or with what made it ineligible changed"; whether another is read is the maintainer's decision, on the pilot's published figures, and not the author's.

What is published on the pull request after the pilot and before any turn of the gated run, for each workload: its K, the three eligibility figures, the two normality tests' p-values, the standard deviation of its medians, each kept model's count of failing draws at that K, the record from the base's map, for `beside` and `besidemt` the base's three watched turns, and every placement that failed, with the check it failed.

### Reading the gated run (`report.mjs`)

Per pair, the change is B / A - 1 in user plus kernel cycles a request.

1. Per placement: the median of its 8 per-pair changes.
2. Across placements: the median of the K_w placement medians and its one-sided 95% upper bound, the j-th of the K_w sorted, j the smallest rank with P(Binomial(K_w, 1/2) >= j) <= 5% (the 20th of 30, the 59th of 100). The lower bound is the (K_w + 1 - j)-th.
3. Resolution, the same for all seven: the A/A is read twice from the same pairs, as recorded and with its lists' labels swapped. If either bound is at or above +1%, or the A/A has fewer than K_w complete placements, the workload is **not resolved**, which does not pass whatever the change's bound.
4. The change on `plain`, `wait`, `close`, `unstaged` or `after` **meets +1%** if its bound across placements is under +1% and that workload's A/A is resolved, and **misses** if its bound is at or above +1% with the A/A resolved. No floor.
5. The change on `beside` or `besidemt` **misses +5%** if its bound across placements is at or above +5% with that workload's A/A resolved, and **meets +5%** if its bound is under +5%, the A/A is resolved and the state is shown (6). No floor on cycles. (A bound of exactly +5% is read as a miss, as the +1% margin reads its own.)
6. On `beside` and `besidemt`, a bound under +5% with the A/A resolved is **not read as declared, which does not pass,** unless the instruction floor of workload 6, b, is met. `report.mjs` writes the figure, and the A/A's own, into its `report.json` for every workload.
7. No reduction is read: the plan declares none, so `report.mjs` reports each workload's bounds and establishes no win.
8. A placement that fails a check, is stopped at its time limit, has other than 8 pairs, or names a binary that is not the plan's leaves its comparison with no bound, which does not pass. No placement is run again, replaced or dropped; the author runs nothing again, and the workload returns to the maintainer. `report.mjs` refuses results that show a placement run again. This plan is not declared again after the run.

Reported as diagnostics, of which a reading uses one alone, the user instructions of the floor in 6: user cycles, kernel cycles, user instructions and user L1 instruction-cache misses a request across placements as cycles are; the spread between placements against the spread within them; each A/A's range of placement medians.
